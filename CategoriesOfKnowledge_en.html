1 Introduction
For me, the term mental training is closely related to learning. Its purpose is to uncover the personal messages hidden in the events of our lives, integrate them at a level appropriate to our current state, and to become aware of and manage the tensions that arise during experiencing and processing. This is learning. However, it immediately becomes apparent that the meaning of the word is not precisely defined, which is an obstacle to resolving the difficulties associated with it. In such cases, it helps a lot to “go back to the stove” a little, to become aware of some basic aspects, which then give greater insight and self-confidence to both the student and the parents, friends, and teachers who want to support him.
To this end, drawing on my experiences as a student, parent, and technical project manager (building an IT system is actually a group learning process in an objective environment, under clear external control, and under deadline pressure), I will divide learning into four clearly distinguishable categories, which I will describe. In the second part, I will expand on the main tasks and supporting ideas related to the categories in a similar division.
1.1 Categories of knowledge/learning
These categories are arbitrarily selected, I do not have the appropriate background knowledge, and others may have already described them better.
1.1.1 Indescribable knowledge
Knowledge for which there is no real textbook is essential, direct, regular contact with a teacher, coach, etc. and personal practice. I include here, for example, the visual arts (painting, sculpture, woodcarving, etc.), vocal and instrumental music, “open-ended” sports (such as traditional martial arts), where the same form of practice can be perfected over a lifetime.
The specific feature of indescribable knowledge is, among other things, that
• It also targets the physical layer of the human body, body awareness.
• You have to rely on physical, and typically external, feedback from an observer, coach or teacher during learning: I can't see myself from the outside, "I have to pay attention to everything at once."
• Practicing builds on both mental and muscle memory: of course I have to remember the notes of the piano piece, but I can't control my entire body, including all ten fingers, at the same time to press each key at the right moment, in the right way, to produce the desired note.
• It is based on the existence of abilities that cannot be influenced by will, their quality may represent an absolute limitation or at least an irreparable disadvantage.
• Nevertheless, there is a huge storehouse of knowledge and underlying mentality that can be acquired, so with proper guidance there is a very good chance of acquiring a satisfactory level of knowledge. Some people would rather look into juggling or tai-chi than karate, and instead of Liszt's virtuosity, they could play romantic pieces or jazz on the piano.
Overall, such knowledge develops the cooperation of physical and mental abilities, and direct external support is essential for learning. Although the basics can be recorded in books, it is still clear that the sentence "I am on page 87 of the Adamy book on Kyokushin karate" is meaningless, this knowledge does not work that way.
1.1.2 Community-shaping knowledge
Neil Postman cites the fundamental difference between oral and written culture in several places. According to the story, in ancient Athens, only those who knew the basic epics by heart were considered literate, and this was therefore a mandatory task for children from wealthier families. Postman points out that such a large amount of text cannot be “learned” in the modern sense. The only solution is for the child to see himself as an actor, identifying with all the characters in the play, and seeing the original text as a reflection of his own thoughts.
Since the epics contained a great deal of human conflict, and the leaders shared this set of experiences almost as personal experiences, they turned to these sources when solving individual problems, and the community practically operated according to the program recorded in this set of legends.
This observation is also reflected in the following quote:
"If a man were permitted to make all the ballads, he need not care who should make the laws of a nation." (Andrew Fletcher, 1703) This is the original version of Rothschild's famous saying "If I could control the money supply of a nation, I need not care who should make the laws"...
However, oral culture also has limitations:
• The myth-set is extremely difficult to change, it cannot follow external events. "Negotiated agreement" between different tribes is almost impossible, the result of the conflict between coded patterns is war, and then the assimilation of myth-sets, as the conquering Roman Empire largely took over the defeated Greek culture.
• It should not exceed the average memory capacity of the participants. What the majority of the group cannot retain will slowly (although certainly with a lot of discussion) be lost.
If these sources can be written off, the situation changes.
• A new work of writing can be born from the new ideas of a single person, and can be preserved unchanged regardless of whether it failed to arouse sufficient interest at the moment of its creation (and thus in the collective memory of the group, usually to the detriment of some other knowledge). At the same time, possible changes in the environment can make this knowledge useful, and the community can prepare for these through the work of innovators.
• The connection becomes weaker, identification with the content read is not equivalent to full role taking. This is both a disadvantage: community members have less trust in their peers' "programmed" functioning, and an advantage: a weaker connection is more negotiable towards different cultures, and may be capable of intellectual integration and cooperation.
Overall, this knowledge is clearly written, and has now grown to a vast extent (literature, art, history). Knowledge of a segment of an appropriate size is essential for belonging to a community, because these determine the common values ​​of a group or society, but at the same time, it is very important to have an outlook, to understand that other cultures exist with different values. This includes “memorized” texts that are retrieved in an unchanged form – in contrast to the previous one, the statement that “I have learned the first five stanzas of the Welsh Bards” or “I am on page 174 in The Sons of the Stone-Hearted Man” is completely correct, this is really suitable for following progress and creating a schedule.
In these cases, it is important to consider the following:
• When learning poetry, a content quote is not enough... the goal of this is to preserve the basic principles of oral culture, emotional identification, typical examples are historical sources, Anthem, Proclamation, National Song, ...
• When choosing required readings, the fundamental aspect is the “creation of a reader”, the experience of the story, the loss of time, the change in the thought process while following the story. This cannot be replaced in any way by the annotation of the “two-page summary”. (Note: I like to play with the word “civilization”, which is not a state, but an activity aimed at creating a civilized person...)
JCR Licklider in his book Libraries of the Future calls this “non-transformable information”: the text carrying knowledge cannot be changed, it must be processed exactly in the given form, because its purpose is to change the processing system, the reader’s thinking, in a way that cannot be defined in advance: the goal is to increase the depth of individual experience and interpretation. This clearly separates it from the following two categories, in which the text is “transformable information”, the goal of which is the unchanging “implantation” of a precisely defined, objective set of knowledge into the reader’s thinking. Such a text can be transformed (with sufficient precision!) without losing its value – a very important conclusion from this is that the knowledge stored in it is actually not a hierarchy of words, sentences and paragraphs, but a set of interconnected statements.
1.1.3 Cooperation rules and knowledge
This could be an instruction manual for a device, a country's civil and criminal code, a hydrographic map or cities of Hungary, an anatomical atlas, or the Road Traffic Law.
• The internal consistency of the text is mandatory, but from the student's perspective it is an external attribute.
• The student does not "interpret" or transform the text, but records it accurately. Unlike a novel, it does not require personal involvement - on the contrary, it demands complete objectivity.
• Regardless, it is irrelevant whether the right-hand rule or the stop sign is introduced first, the knowledge is “simultaneous”,
To acquire such knowledge, accuracy is essential, the full set of knowledge must be available, and the ability to select and apply the rule that best fits a given situation must be present. Similar to the previous category, progress is proportional to the amount of text reviewed - however, the order is not always mandatory, the larger chapters only refer to each other.
1.1.4 Understanding-based knowledge
I consider this to be the part remaining after the previous categories, the purpose of which is to know building blocks and rules (precision), to assess unknown situations based on these, to plan and implement interventions (creativity). This includes mathematics and physics, the engineering knowledge that relies on them, etc., in English STEM.
The related teaching material is typically textual, perhaps illustrated with images and videos, but this only helps understanding. For example, in the case of an astronomy subject, photographs and diagrams are clearly useful, but the same knowledge can clearly be conveyed only in the form of text (including numbers and formulas, of course). The opposite is not true: even if a diagram or photograph depicts the same elements, it is not possible to recover positions or orbital equations from it, for that the text is needed.
In this area, precise memorization and constant application similar to the previous one are not enough, progress cannot really be linked to the amount of text read, or even recalled without error. The purpose of the curriculum is to provide continuous guidance on knowledge, and learning is to “highlight” interconnected insights from the text or from the teacher’s live explanation. It happens that the student, knowing the previously processed part, can infer in advance what has not yet been read, and this (for example, in comparison with a novel) is not a subjective, but an objective measure of his knowledge. The student’s practical task is to independently rearrange, criticize, and search for contradictions (in comparison with all previous categories).
It is important to add that the maintenance and necessary further development of the technological infrastructure that surrounds us and fundamentally determines our quality of life primarily depends on this knowledge. Relying on literacy, the knowledge accumulated here far exceeds the comprehension capacity of any individual, so we divide it into levels, learning different physics at the primary, secondary and higher education levels. All this poses a serious challenge for all participants.
1.2 Acquiring and using knowledge based on understanding
The problem is often that the student has uncertain knowledge. He remembers the book, the diagrams, recalls the words, but somehow the “ability to see” is missing. Some tasks work, others don’t, and it’s not really possible to identify where the shortcomings are. Therefore, it is useful to be aware of a few basic aspects.
1.2.1 “Knowledge elements”
This knowledge consists of a network of precisely defined, identifiable elements, like a deck of cards. The cards can be divided into different groups and have different roles, and each task will require a part of the deck. If this does not work, the cards causing the problem must be found and their content clarified and made usable.
1.2.2 Terms of technical language
These are the words that we can use when describing a task. In the case of geometry, these are plane, line, point, angle, ..., in physics, mass, speed, etc. In each subject area, we deal with a specific subset - for example, in elementary school mathematics, the concept of a natural number is introduced, later in geometry, they encounter angles that can be described with numbers, and then in physics, they encounter a small car rolling down a slope. It is very important to clearly identify basic concepts and use their precise definitions, as "I don't understand" and "I don't know" often hide uncertainty about such concepts.
To check and increase confidence, situation description exercises can be performed, the goal can be to translate a scene or figure into professional, accurate text, or to draw a figure from text. When this goes well, it is worth continuing with incomplete or contradictory tasks, the goal of which is active participation, accepting that there may be mistakes in the task. This is especially important during self-control, which is a great challenge, natural behavior is more like covering up one's own mistakes, the reverse of which we are trying to incorporate here.
1.2.3 Operations
With the help of actions, we change a described situation. So these are “cards” that change something in the situation described with the previous tools. It is important to know that this is again a big challenge: instead of the natural thinking “I will solve it somehow” operation, here I can perform strict, controlled interventions. It is like chess, for example, where physically any piece can be placed anywhere, but the rules only allow certain moves; it requires serious concentration, a game consists of many moves, and it is easy to lose patience. Accordingly, the actions are also introduced.
In the first step, we “practice walking in place”, for example, converting between previously introduced units of measurement. At this point, we become aware that, depending on the way of description, different numbers can mean the same thing. Then come the “identical transformations”, when we change several points of the described system so that the previous state remains – we realize that this is also possible. Then come the questions “what happens if...” (passive prediction), and finally “what do we need to do so that...” (active intervention.
It is worth being aware of the constantly increasing difficulty, and in case of uncertainty, stepping back and practicing the previous levels. Here too, it is important that the student encounters incorrect tasks, in the form of insufficient or contradictory data, or unattainable goals. Continuous monitoring and active error detection can only be practiced in this way, and one must get used to the resulting internal tension.
1.2.4 Critical thinking
The tools listed are fully transferable to any field of expertise, as well as to everyday life, because in fact we acquire the tools of critical thinking through the use of a precisely defined, objectively verifiable sub-area. Today, when we receive information of unverifiable quality from so many sources, it is of paramount importance that we are able to:
• Be critical of the news we receive, and of "pre-packaged" or emotionally based opinions.
• We honestly accept our own limitations in both the depth of our knowledge and the precision of our analytical skills. Let there be a few areas in which we are confident, and based on this we assess the level of our knowledge in other areas.
• Let us be open and welcome (sometimes not without clouding) joy when we find an error in our own derivation, because by correcting it we can achieve a better result - which we would not have a chance to achieve otherwise.
2 Supporting thoughts
The characteristics of the categories make it clear that although we are talking about “learning” or related questions and uncertainties in each of them, they function fundamentally differently and require different kinds of support. In the absence of deeper experience, I can list more general thoughts and my own experiences for the first three categories, but I have a few tried and tested methods supported by theory regarding the acquisition of understanding-based knowledge / application of critical thinking.
2.1 General advice
All of these are related to the concept of "planning" to some extent, about which it is also worth recalling a few clichés.
1. We do not make a plan to implement it, but to have something to deviate from. Every activity is a kind of learning, nothing happens exactly as imagined, some level of adaptation is always needed. Making a preliminary plan is extremely important to keep these changes under control as much as possible, to avoid "drift" when the compromises made one after another make the original goal unattainable, and to have an exit point when (despite all the emotional storms, relatively) we can make an objective decision to abandon the activity and minimize losses.
2. This sounds really good in its original form: “Failing to plan is planning to fail” – a clear formulation of the negative side of the previous point. If there is no plan that clearly and in advance defines the roles, cooperation and responsibilities of the implementers, then the process turns into a drag on these instead of the original goal.
This is all the more true for learning, as its sole purpose is to integrate previously unknown knowledge and acquire skills - on the one hand, this makes planning extremely difficult, but on the other hand, the experiences gained during learning related to planning will be of enormous value when we prepare an activity based on the knowledge we have already (or perhaps just now) acquired.
Learning conflicts with natural human motivations in many ways, such as seeking and acknowledging our own mistakes, or abandoning the level we have already reached. We often have to struggle with ourselves to acquire new knowledge.
2.1.1 Parent-child-school relationship
One of the most common forms of the learning process, and perhaps the most defining in a person's life, is when we as parents try to support our children's school education. It can be said without any hesitation: this is very difficult. It is worth fixing a few cornerstones and adhering to them consistently (as parents, in our own behavior!) in order to ensure that learning turns into a conscious developmental process and not a drift during our child's school career.
• Don't expect your child to love learning and going to school. It's completely understandable that they will use any means at their disposal to extort any concessions. Accept this with great love, acknowledge their creativity and enthusiasm – and then take them to school. Don't bargain, don't give them gifts, make it clear that this is mandatory for them, just as we go to work and complete the tasks assigned to us, regardless of how enthusiastic we are about it at the time. In this way, we benefit others, and we enjoy the results of their work when the water flows from the tap or when we shop in the store. Instead of expectations, we accept a shared destiny.
• At the same time, let's pay attention to every real need and give them a choice. For example, they need to bring new pencils, erasers, and watercolors to drawing class from now on. Don't sigh and say, "Let's get them what they need," but go together, choose together, discuss why (this includes the fact that it shouldn't cost twice as much because it's a superhero picture...), listen to the arguments, school stories. It's not a lot of extra time, it's important for the child to have acted as a guide in this area, and they'll remember the conversation when they use the tools. Instead of being restricted, an extremely important communication channel opens up if we're really there with them and take the time - which is the only way we can do it, and we'll miss it a lot if we miss it now.
• The child does not need sympathy, but support. Although the hero role may be attractive at the moment, neither the curriculum nor the teacher will change if we agree with our child's complaint and start scolding them. Education is in a very bad situation on a global level in terms of tools, resources, manpower, etc., and it is completely pointless to hold a teacher accountable for this, who is obviously suffering even more than our child. It is harder, but in the long run much more useful to put down the hatchet and grab a pencil, always walking two steps behind and struggling with the solution together with the child. Let him enjoy the joy of success, the feeling of "I showed it to dad" is a great feeling, he needs this in order to start the next task. If he gets really stuck, you can also guide him in the right direction with questions, it is much better than impatiently pointing out the wrong number or the ignored condition - for which you should not blame him, but rather tell your own similar stories. And perhaps most importantly: when we are faced with a task, it will take over the management of the situation from us. Do we get frustrated or do we take a deep breath, take a step back and try again? Do we evaluate the answers after a Google search – or do we type the ChatGP answer without thinking, as is the custom today? (it is not allowed, don't even pick it up!!!). And if all the ropes break, can you agree that you need to show the teacher how far you have come, where your enthusiasm has run out, let him show you the solution – in the best case, the teacher will be in the game and help. Handling failure while learning is one of the most important lessons, incorporating the right patterns is decisive, the time invested now will pay off many times over later. In a lucky case, a family division of labor is established, father is good at this, mother is good at that, and the child sees this dynamic working in an environment that is important to him.

2.1.2 Goals and framework / motivation and resources
At the beginning of our career, we receive the goals and framework of most of our learning processes from outside in the form of "mandatory curriculum", a smaller part is chosen freely (sports, professional circles, etc.). The ratio of these shifts later, as we try to fit in studies that are interesting from a personal or career perspective alongside our daily work and perhaps family. Regardless of the environment, it is worth examining these four cornerstones before starting the learning process, or in the case of freely chosen studies, before making a decision.
It is worth examining the goal by breaking it down into specific results. Instead of "I should get at least a B in Hungarian", spend a few hours on what are the required readings, what is the period, and how many artists I need to learn details about. It is obviously useful if this happens in class, but if not, you can also go around at home.
After this, you can think through the framework more precisely: when and what kind of assessment can be expected, when you need to acquire the Black Diamonds, which subject seems easier, and during which period you may need to study together.
Resources can already be allocated for this: parents can take out their own calendars, prepare to spend some time around November, perhaps ask about progress, talk about the plot, because this way this experience will remain instead of the week and a half of panic and confusion before the paper. We look at when the small concert will be at the music school, and plan time and peace of mind for that (the effect of practicing under stress extends to the entire family).
And finally, there are two forms of motivation. One is related to external requirements, there is no alternative here, the student must be “sold” that it will benefit him. In the case of freely chosen studies, both the goal and the framework are more flexible, let's think about the personal or professional significance of the goal they want to achieve and build on that. In such an environment, it is essential that the time and cost invested so far are not a sufficient argument for continuing studies, in which case burnout can be masked, often in a completely different area (work, family). The advantage of freely chosen studies is that they can be discontinued or continued later, and the experience gained often returns the investment in another area.
2.1.3 Assessment of the current situation
Here too we find four parts.
• What I think I know. This is the most pleasant, the “sure foundation” on which I will build my current studies. This also gives it its danger: I want to see it as big as possible (since I got an A from it, the preventive panic learning and the lack of elaboration of half of the items are covered by the beneficial darkness of oblivion). If it is important, it is worth asking for external review and accepting helpful criticism, it feels bad at that moment, but it avoids much bigger problems. As Mark Twain said: “It ain't what you don't know that gets you into trouble. It's what you know for sure that just ain't so.“ (It's not what you don't know that gets you into trouble, it's what you are sure of but not so.)
• What I don't know that I know. It seems like a bit of a cheap marketing ploy, but this is the other side of asking for external help: it often turns out that some of the "dumb items" mentioned in the previous example didn't work out just because of too much stress and a few minor misunderstandings, after clarifying these, you can move on to the known knowledge part with a calm mind. When this happens, proper celebration is very important, which makes it easier to use external help and accept criticism for the next task.
• What I know I don't know. This is the simplest category, as this is why I start learning, the parts of the goal and the path that I know are found here, we analyzed this in the previous chapter. Existing knowledge and possibly external support (such as a teacher) help to find the critical points.
• What I don't know is that I don't know. This is naturally the hardest part, fortunately during "normal studies" the teacher is aware of these, this is the harder part of his task: to point out previously unseen shortcomings to the student in a way that doesn't make him lose motivation. If we encounter this at home, as parents (our child sadly says how stupid he is, and the others do it much better), then it is worth making two things aware. 1: how much he had to understand in order to see this new area. 2: don't be afraid of it, the teacher's job is precisely to guide him step by step, and in the end he will understand it in the same way as everything he has learned so far. You can perhaps discuss these with the teacher, so that it can be brought to the previous, "I know that I don't know" level. When solving a real problem, this "mumus" category, here brainstorming that reveals the different background knowledge of the participants, can be useful. Everyone has a different blind spot, and during the consultation, the outlines of the area, the missing expertise and possible sources of acquiring it can be formed.
2.1.4 Creating a strategic time plan
In the previous points, we managed to identify subtasks, estimate their difficulty, and now we just need to arrange them in order. However, this is preceded by another question that we usually have difficulty asking ourselves: how sure am I in estimating the difficulty? The method is simple: I have to strictly go through the task, how long it will take, what exactly I am doing, and whether I can really do it (I can't prepare for my trumpet exam during the two-week vacation). In the process, it may turn out that I am not able to make this detailed plan, I still have to study for it, and in addition to the "difficulty" and the expected time requirement, "confidence" is added, 95% in usual tasks, let's say 50% in an unknown area. On the other hand, I see the dependencies: if this uncertain task "slips", which of the other tasks will it affect (I can't start it or go beyond a given point).
All of these together make up the real weight of the tasks, now I can really start setting the order – and I run into another obstacle: especially in the case of bigger challenges, it is tempting to think that “I will gain self-confidence by solving a few simple tasks”. This is not how it works. The biggest enemy of self-confidence is a difficult task swept under the rug, the simpler ones often depend on it, which actually only increases the stress, which I would try to manage by solving a few more simple tasks. The source of the related quote is Randy Pausch’s lecture on Time Management: “If you have to eat a frog, don't spend a lot of time looking at it first. And if you have to eat three of them, don't start with the small one.” (“If you have to eat a frog, don’t spend too much time looking at it. And if you have to eat three, don’t start with the smallest one.”) Difficult, uncertain, and tasks that affect many other elements should be placed at the top of the list. Safety in the schedule is helped by breaking down the subtasks better and creating more safety reserves.
Then we deal with placing them in the calendar, working from the back to the front. Before the exam, paper, handover, etc., we leave a reserve of between two days and two weeks, depending on the size of the task. This is independent of the individual time reserve assigned to the subtasks, depending on uncertainty, and is intended to familiarize ourselves with the achieved result (preparation, project, etc.). Up to this point, we have dealt with it as a student or creator, now we think of ourselves as a presenter - this is the time for exam preparation.
The time plan should of course only be used as a guide during implementation, it informs us about how accurate the preliminary analysis was (we can get it wrong at first, but with experience this is just one of the skills that can be consciously developed), and we can become aware much earlier if the plan has really slipped. Therefore, we can ask for help for specific problems well before the deadline, instead of trying to save a hopelessly slipped preparation under constant stress, instead of sleeping, typically with minimal efficiency (the idea of ​​the latter can help us gain motivation to implement the former).
2.1.5 Daily schedule
Maybe it's not just me who is familiar with the fact that I've been preparing for the exam for "days", but I can't remember where I am and what happened to that time. In such cases, the pomodoro method, which is named after a kitchen timer, can help. According to this, I divide the day into time slots, for example, 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes of break. During work, I don't look at my phone, the internet, etc. because the 5-minute break will soon be there, but when I'm not working, I take a little walk, have a drink, check my messages, etc.
For example, I plan six such half-hour sessions for the morning and six for the afternoon. In the morning, I divide the tasks planned in the strategic time plan into such sessions, fill the day (which in the case of a more complex task could be the first 25 minutes of work), and at the end (or in the last 25 minutes) I evaluate how well I managed to implement the plan, and if I slipped up, what was the reason. It is worth keeping a journal of these in the first period to make the processing of experiences more efficient.
By following the method, progress becomes clearer and I can better plan the remaining time, which usually helps a lot in reducing stress.
2.1.6 Involving a helper
My sons have taught me the saying that no one completes BME alone. The above methods help a lot, but they do not guarantee that all plans will be implemented; the higher the level of learning, the greater the chance that we will encounter seemingly insurmountable obstacles. It is important to be aware of the following: 1: this is still a natural part of the learning process; 2: the sooner we ask for help, the sooner we will find our way back to the planned path; our efficiency usually decreases when we slip up; and 3: the above methods quickly identify the person we are turning to, whether it is a group of “fellow students,” an acquaintance who has passed us by, or a tutor.
2.1.7 Exam preparation
The exam naturally causes stress, so it not only checks the existence of knowledge, but also whether it can be reliably recalled under pressure. Later, when we have to apply it in a critical situation while solving a real task, without external attention and control, it is better not to try this first. And it is better if those who were examined by strict teachers at the time are put in such a situation, they are less likely to make mistakes with serious consequences. If I want to be such a person, I must be able to pay the price.
But whatever I can do to reduce stress is worth doing.
“It is mandatory to write a rifle, it is forbidden to use it”. What is a rifle? A concise list of key phrases and basic definitions, from which the content of the assignment can be developed either orally or in writing, if the task is of that nature. Preparing a rifle is serious mental work: I have to review the entire material, set an order of importance, highlight the most important ones – by the time it is completed, I will have a much better understanding of the assignment. Therefore, it is not worth asking others for a rifle, although comparing them can also be a useful element of preparation. The description is important because the number, location, length, etc. of the lines are also fixed as an image and breaks the “panic of a blank white paper”: I imagine them there or even write them down (in the case of a draft or verbal outline), then I explain them in writing, and if I write them orally, I write additional reminder words, years, etc. next to them. From there, there can be no big problem...
Exams usually last longer than a regular paper or answer, which seems like a lot in advance, but sitting indoors we often get stuck on time. It helps a lot if we practice the exam at home, even several times, knowing the conditions. For oral exams, we say the answer out loud with a blank piece of paper, after a few minutes of preparation, or even record it and listen to it again. This doesn't go well the first time, but after the third, it becomes habitual - and that's the point. For written exams, we work through the time allotted for the exam in roughly the same amount of time without interruption. At the beginning, we spend a suitable amount of time (5-15 minutes) reviewing the tasks: how much time we will spend on each, approximately how much we will write, how confident we are in our answers. The goal is not to be flawless, but to get the most points, so we use our strengths, but we don't spend all the time on them, because you also have to write something for a few points for weaker tasks. At the end, we also leave 5-15 minutes for checking, when we read through each task and answer once more, correcting any minor errors. If I see a big problem that I don't have time to fix, and I'm sure I gave a wrong answer, I write it down, but I don't try to fix it.
I play out the exam. I imagine the whole day, when I get up, when I get dressed, when I get there, where I'm going (I visit a foreign location beforehand or look it up on an internet street map), how long I'll have to wait, will there be anyone I know there, what I'll do with my time? I take my rifles, a book, an atlas, or the materials I've downloaded and arranged on my phone or tablet (if there's a place to put them during the exam), this prevents last-minute panic that I don't know something, because I can quickly look them up.
By the time I sit for the exam, the environment, constraints, and conditions are familiar to me, which reduces stress - leaving me with more energy for the task.
2.2 Indescribable knowledge
This form of learning will relatively rarely be the basis of a livelihood, but rather a quality pastime that takes you away from everyday life, adds a different depth to life, and allows you to build human relationships. Regardless, many skills and experiences will have a direct impact on our development and professional performance.
In our time, school education is the norm, although the average class size has decreased, expectations are high, and there is not much chance of establishing a direct, informal relationship between teacher and student. In contrast, in a fine arts club, the teacher is an artist himself, a karate coach has of course been training for decades, etc., thus providing a direct role model for the child. His personality is therefore extremely important – it means much more than the results achieved in competitions or even the specific field of education. It is worth visiting several places and choosing one from which the child comes home with positive experiences and makes objectively assessable progress.
In the case of group learning, the atmosphere of the circle is also significant, do the older ones help and support the beginners, do they enjoy and look forward to being together? It may happen that this level does not work, in which case it is worth considering switching regardless of the results achieved so far. The acquired skills are not lost, but a negative environment can have a lasting influence on the personality.
In the case of learning aimed at physical abilities, the connection between practice and development is much clearer. If you can get the child to prepare regularly and with concentration, even if it is only twenty minutes a day at first (and not try to imitate progress or explain failure during the lesson), after a short time he will also notice that what he only imagined before, he now knows, and tomorrow he will be bored and looking for a new task. This experience is decisive and (with a little struggle) can be transferred to school tasks as well.
At one point, I realized that even though I was physically present because of my job, we spent little time doing activities with my sons, so I looked for and enrolled in karate training. I think it was very beneficial. They saw that I followed the coach's instructions in the same way that I expected them to do, and the coach expected them to do it, there was no difference between us. They saw how I struggled with my own physical limitations, that I also had to get out to breathe from time to time, and of course they would beat me in circles when I was relaxed – it must have meant a lot to them that they could be better than me on equal terms, and that I was visibly proud of it was not a problem. None of them achieved great success and over time we stopped karate, but their physical abilities, fitness, self-discipline and self-esteem were fundamentally supported by these few years.
All three of my sons (albeit with different enthusiasm) went through music school, learned solfège and piano, two of them still play it today. This form of learning is much closer to the school environment, sheet music is a special text, learning it develops memory - and music theory, singing, etc. connects them to cultural heritage, they don't run away from a concert, they listen to a variety of musical genres, finding different joy in each. Performing at "mini concerts" is especially important, because here they have to experience and overcome a very high level of stress. As they progress, they have to play increasingly difficult and longer pieces, and they have to wait longer and longer for their turn. Of course, I would have tried to save them from this if I saw that they were in a negative spiral, but they always performed well. Later, I could calmly draw their attention to a performance, exam, etc. in relation to the fact that they had already proven this ability – and this sometimes gave them an advantage over their contemporaries.
2.3 Community-shaping knowledge
If we had to describe our world today with one word, the adjective “noisy” would probably come first. An incredible amount of information is pouring in at us every moment through our portable devices, and a “constant active online presence” is almost an expectation. In the continuous flood, it is almost impossible to select the elements that are important to us and need to be processed from those that some source sends us just to keep our attention for a few more seconds, or perhaps to direct our opinion and worldview towards the interests of the client. This is a difficult task even for adults, not to mention children, whose thinking is built into defense, filtering, and superficial reactions from a very young age, crowding out involvement and their own imagination. After this, we have no particular basis to expect them to be able to pay attention, “stay calm,” or even like to read for a longer period of time.
Fortunately, the solution is also at our fingertips, and apart from the last few decades, it has determined the lives of children. These are traditional children's games, dances (indescribable knowledge, manual dexterity, body control, sense of rhythm), folk songs (oral culture, "memorizer"), fairy tales and legends (written culture). Group games teach self-discipline and adherence to rules, a school environment will seem familiar. Those who have learned to sing and perhaps hum folk songs during some activity will find it much easier to learn the verse, as they already have experience in memorizing and recalling the lyrics. And the evening story does not necessarily have to mean a tablet and a YouTube playlist, it also works if the father lies down in the room, first (and with many repetitions if desired) reads the 77 Hungarian folk tales by Gyula Illyés, and the beautiful reed lady by Elek Benedek night after night.
The evening routine, early and restful sleep are immediate help, and the need for one's own reading skills appears, while the books are changed, for example, István Komjáthy's Mondák book, Mór Jókai's and Gyula Verne's novels are introduced. The "world-famous Hungarians" grew up on these stories. It also requires time from the parents, but the evening regime has just as good an effect on them as it does on the children.
Where these foundations are lacking, learning to read poetry, actually reading the required readings, requires more support. Here, the parent's persistence ("why is it good, why do we have to torture the poor child with this?") can actually be supported with the message that poems and (the classic, not by chance included in the curriculum) short stories and novels directly affect the child's brain during reading, learning, thinking, and conversation, building lessons, attitudes, and values, without which it is much more difficult to become a responsible, value-creating adult. The school deserves every support in helping our own child in this - even if he or she does not want this at a given moment.
2.4 Cooperation rules, knowledge
At this point, the direction of processing the source is reversed: as a student, I do not have to adapt to the material received (be it a formal exercise, a score or a play), but I have to actively extract from it the knowledge expected of me. Even at the level of rules, the task is not the ability to repeat verbatim, but the selection and application of appropriate elements in a given situation. It is advisable to make this operation a clear part of the learning process and to use appropriate tools.
A convenient solution can be to create “knowledge cards”. I invented this during my university years (obviously not the first), and later it proved to be very useful in system design. Instead of notebooks or A4 sheets, I fold the A4 sheet in four, write on the top what will be on it, and then I start to note my thoughts or what was said during the conversation or presentation. Of course, I start to fill in several such A6 cards at once, and I also take out previous cards when new knowledge related to the topic on them appears. It obviously takes some getting used to at first, and the size of the card may prove to be small – there is nothing wrong with opening several cards numbered for one topic.
However, our brains adapt surprisingly quickly to tools, and here it gets exactly the help it needs to master “transformable information”. You soon develop a sense that the short notes for the title really fit on a card, when the conversation or thought process strays from the original topic to create a new card. Of course, the cards can be rearranged later, merged or split up while retaining the knowledge, and the old ones can fit in a box (or even in an envelope by month), in case there is something interesting on them.
This obviously sounds like there should be an IT tool for managing knowledge cards. Well, yes, Douglas Engelbart introduces and describes the above system under the name notecard in his 1962 report “Augmenting Human Intellect”. Apple’s software suite included the HyperCard application that supported it between 1987 and 1998. The question arises: if we can publish and access anything on the Internet, then these cards, with the appropriate server background, would be suitable for managing global human knowledge. This system was described by Ted Nelson under the name Xanadu – to him we can also thank the concepts of “informatics” and “hyperlink”. The prototype of Xanadu was released essentially at the same time as the World Wide Web service we know today, but unfortunately it did not meet market expectations (and in my opinion contained three fundamental errors that contributed to its failure).
Regardless of this, there are Mindmap support systems that usually allow you to manage short notes, organize them into a reference network, group them, color them, etc. in a browser. In the long run, it is of course more convenient to use such software, since the content is available anywhere, anytime (depending on the device), the same card can be displayed in multiple lists, searched for, or even used during interrogation. On the other hand, every tool has its own weaknesses, it is easy to get lost among the many options, you may have to pay for more interesting services, etc. It is worth choosing one whose free version is already “good enough” and includes a service for downloading your own mindmaps in some meaningful format. For this, it is worth finding an IT specialist or a friend who uses such a tool and is satisfied with it.
Why is this good? I mean, why does a child have to struggle with blind maps, dates, and names of kings? The main aspect when choosing the teaching material is that with their help we create people who are better informed in the world, less likely to be misled or offended, and who recognize such experiments sooner, for example, if they have a historical background (this is how more flat-earthers than necessary, those looking for chips in vaccines, and most recently AI enthusiasts, preach their words). Mastering the learning method based on knowledge cards is also very important. The brain stores knowledge in a networked form at the physiological level (neuronal network segments), and raising the organization to a skill level is practicing abstract thinking with the help of an external tool. Not to mention the previously mentioned exam preparation, where instead of a pile of books, you can start with a network of short notes that refer precisely to the important parts.
2.5 Understanding-based knowledge
The infrastructure that sustains our civilization is built on this knowledge. It is identical in structure and learning methodology to the previous set of rules, only its size is much larger. The goal of learning in this area is to develop our brains through literally decades of continuous work, thereby being able to safely apply, possibly teach, and at the highest level expand the knowledge accumulated by our predecessors who followed the same path and reached the highest level so far.
There are two obstacles that test us on this path. One is that we get the hang of it, we want to do very well in every field – but we simply have human limitations on the amount of material we can absorb. We have to accept the “good enough” level in other fields in order to be able to offer the maximum of our abilities in the field related to our goals. The other problem arises here: this knowledge can only be built up step by step, during the learning we will be confronted with the fact that the previous maximum was only enough to get to the threshold of the next level, where we will need even greater performance to move on. Surviving the exam period in a high-level university program only entitles me to meet professors who have spent their entire lives in that one field through the next semester’s subjects. They will completely ignore my parallel subjects and try to cram as much knowledge into my head as possible so that they can pass the exam and maybe find a student or two with whom they would like to work later. No one's goal here is the well-being of every student, and a well-functioning higher education system also has the task of rejecting students who are not prepared for this level of workload. Good intentions, enthusiasm and self-sacrifice are necessary, but they are not enough in themselves to become a doctor, engineer or teacher.
However – there is no need for it. Unfortunately, today’s world of “winners” and “the rest” makes it very difficult to recognize this, but in reality we all want to match our own complex set of abilities and motivations with the equally complex expectations of the world. The 90 / 9 / 1 rule helps us to do this.
• In 90% of the areas I am involved in, I have 1% of the available knowledge, and this is enough to use them safely. This is how I relate to my household appliances: I read and follow their instructions, or to my car: I know and follow the traffic rules to the necessary depth, and I pay attention to the vehicle's behavior and indicator lights.
• Continuing with the car example, 9% are car mechanics, to whom owners take their vehicles when they are not behaving properly, and they have the expertise, tools and spare parts to restore them to a condition suitable for safe driving.
• The 1% are those who design the cars and test them on test tracks under extreme conditions to ensure their reliability.
From this, it can be seen how one-dimensional and useless the patterns that I try to copy from, for example, elite sports or movies, or now influencer videos, are in my own life. Each person chooses the areas they consider important, their abilities, preparedness, processed and unprocessed experiences, etc. determine the maximum that can be achieved, but compromises must also be made when allocating the finite time available. The ultimate goal is to find communities that are supportive not only from a professional perspective, but also from a human perspective, that demand and value our personal abilities, talents, and preparedness, which we put at their service during our working hours.
So why do we work on acquiring knowledge based on understanding? Not so that we know everything about everything, but because we experience it as a fulfillment as we know more and more in the area that interests us. We do not experience this level as how many people we know more than, but because it has made higher levels accessible to us. It is tempting to think that one day we can “reach the top”, but it is much more realistic to think that we increasingly see those communities and those roles within them in which we could live happily, doing valuable work. So when the phase of our lives spent primarily on learning comes to an end, we can choose between these, and not stand in a line from which they will choose.
